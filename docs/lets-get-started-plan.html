The job

Three jobs, one page, in a deliberate order

A welcome page usually fails by trying to be a brochure. This one has measurable work to do, and the order the work appears in is the main design decision on the page. Putting it before signup changes who each job is aimed at, but not what the jobs are.

Activation

Get to first value fast

Menumo is worthless until there is a menu and a day of sales in it. Every analytics surface reads from useAnalyticsSnapshot, which is empty on day one. The checklist exists to fill it.

Expectation

Say what beta means

Several pages exist but are not routed yet. If a user hears about a feature and cannot find it, that is a support ticket and a trust dent. Naming what is not built yet costs nothing and buys patience.

Commercial

Make the plan legible

There is no free tier. Trial users need to know what happens on day 15 before day 14, or the trial ends as a surprise wall instead of a decision.

The ordering call: capability first, price last. The page opens with what Menumo does and closes with what it costs. Leading with a pricing table suppresses both signup and activation. Price is never hidden though - it is one anchor click away in the sub-nav, and the beta strip sits above everything.
Why pre-signup is the stronger position: the beta status board is the whole argument. Told after signup, "scheduling isn't built yet" reads as a bait and switch. Told before, it reads as candour, and it filters out the operator who was only ever going to churn over the missing feature. The people who sign up anyway have already accepted the gaps, which is a far better trial cohort. Pricing works the same way: nobody reaches day 15 surprised.

The two shortcuts

Already sold skips stage 2

The hero keeps its Start free trial button pointing straight at /auth. Someone who arrived from a recommendation should not be made to read a brief first. They rejoin at stage 4, where the checklist catches them.

Returning customer skips 2 and 4

Log in goes to /auth and then to /dashboard. They only see /get-started again if they open it themselves, or if the sidebar counter is still showing an unfinished checklist.

One route, two states

Visitor public

  • Landing chrome: top nav, no sidebar
  • Hero: "Here's what you're getting"
  • Checklist is a static preview with time estimates, no ticks
  • Every tier button reads "Start free trial" and carries the plan through

Signed in personalized

  • App chrome: DashboardLayout sidebar
  • Hero greets by name and shows the live trial counter
  • Checklist derived from real collections and tickable
  • Current plan outlined; buttons become "Keep Pro" and "Switch"

The shell swap is already solved in this codebase. AppShell renders bare children for paths in PUBLIC_PATHS and wraps everything else in DashboardLayout. For this one route the branch keys off auth state rather than the path, which is a few lines in an existing file.

"Doesn't an extra page cost signups?" It would, if it were an extra page. It is not: FeaturesPage.tsx, PricingPage.tsx and SubscriptionPricingPage.tsx are all zero-byte files sitting in src/pages/ right now. Three routes were staked out for exactly this content and never built. /get-started is those three pages, merged into the one place a prospect actually looks. And the hero's direct trial button stays, so nobody who is ready gets slowed down.

Why each block sits where it does

A

Beta strip, first. Context before any promise. Persistent, not dismissible on this page - it is the page's honesty contract. Elsewhere in the app it is dismissible per session.

B

Named greeting, named truck. Both are already on the account record. It costs one interpolation and it is the difference between a product and a form.

C

Checklist above features. The highest-value thing on the page. Exactly one step carries the coral button - a single next action, never five equal ones.

D

Features named by outcome. "Food cost per item", not "menu module". The mockup once printed each tile's route as a caption on the theory that it taught navigation - that was wrong and is not shipped. Nobody navigates a sidebar app by URL, so a route in the copy is just an implementation detail on show. The route lives in the link target; the card says "Open".

E

Three columns, no spin. Naming the unbuilt things is what makes the "live" column believable. This is the block that earns the beta.

F

Plans last, trial card marked. The current plan is outlined rather than a "most popular" flag - the user is already on Pro, so the honest emphasis is on what they would lose by dropping.

G

Feedback at the exit - designed, not shipped. Asking after they have seen the gaps gets specific reports instead of vague ones. Held back until there is a real destination: a promise of a same-day reply behind a dead mailto costs more trust than it earns. See the open question below.

Beta framing

How honest to be, and the exact words

What we say

  • "Your data is real and kept." The single biggest beta fear is that the work gets wiped. Kill it in the first line.
  • "Some features are still landing - we say which." Then actually say which, in block E. A vague admission is worse than none.
  • "Beta accounts get a direct line." Trades polish for access. That is a fair deal and operators recognize it as one.

What we avoid

  • Apologizing. "Sorry, we're still in beta" invites the reader to lower their estimate of the whole product.
  • Dated promises. "Scheduling ships in August" becomes a broken promise the moment it slips. Ship order, not dates.
  • Hiding beta below the fold. If they discover it from a bug instead of from us, the trust cost is far higher.
Needs your sign-off before it ships: a founding-rate promise - something like "beta accounts keep this price for 12 months after we leave beta" - is the strongest reason to subscribe during beta rather than wait. It is also a binding commercial commitment, so it is deliberately absent from the mockup. Tell me the terms and I will write it in.

Build notes

What it takes to ship this

New files

  • src/pages/GetStartedPage.tsx - the route, branching on auth state
  • src/components/onboarding/ - checklist, status board, tier cards
  • src/hooks/useOnboardingProgress.ts - derives the 5 ticks from real collections
  • src/config/plans.ts - already called for by the subscription plan; both pages read it
  • src/config/betaStatus.ts - the three status columns as data, so updating them is a one-line edit

Files to change

  • routes/AppRoutes.tsx - add /get-started as a public route, no guard
  • layout/DashboardLayout.tsx - nav item with counter; fix the dead Settings and Help links
  • pages/LandingPage.tsx - relabel the nav CTA and repoint 5 of its 8 /auth links
  • layout/AppShell.tsx - /get-started picks its shell by auth state, not by PUBLIC_PATHS
  • pages/AuthPage.tsx - read ?plan and ?from, redirect back to /get-started
  • pages/DashboardPage.tsx - resume card while incomplete
  • models/account.ts + schema.cue - add onboardingCompletedAt
Sequencing: the public state ships first and alone. It reads src/config/plans.ts and src/config/betaStatus.ts and touches no account data, so it has zero dependency on the entitlement layer or on the tier rename. That makes it the cheapest useful thing in the backlog: it closes three empty routes and gives the landing page somewhere to point. The signed-in state follows once onboardingCompletedAt exists.
Delete on the way past: src/pages/FeaturesPage.tsx, src/pages/PricingPage.tsx and src/pages/SubscriptionPricingPage.tsx are zero-byte and unrouted. Once /get-started exists they are dead weight that will confuse the next person reading the tree. Remove them in the same PR.
